What our word lists reveal about 4–7 letter words

Does a five-letter puzzle usually contain two vowels? Are repeated letters actually rare? Which first and final letters appear most often? We tested those questions against the word files that power WordUnscramble.eu rather than borrowing a generic list of puzzle tips.

The important limitation: these are properties of this site's available word data, not the probabilities of an official Wordle answer, a random English sentence, or a tournament Scrabble rack. A dictionary includes many inflected and uncommon words, and its editorial choices affect every percentage.

What was counted

We took the alphabetic entries of exactly 4, 5, 6 and 7 letters from the site's Broad English length files (data/w4.txt through data/w7.txt). We compared each entry with the site's data/common.txt membership list. The resulting Common English subsets are what the tables below analyze. Each word is counted once for its word length, not weighted by how frequently speakers use it.

The Common subset is proportionally smaller for longer words: about 30.7% of the Broad four-letter entries appear in Common, compared with 10.6% for seven-letter entries. This is a property of the datasets' overlap, not a statement that a seven-letter word is inherently “less English”. When searching for an ordinary puzzle answer, start with Common English in Word Finder; switch to Broad if the puzzle accepts rare vocabulary.

Finding 1: repeated letters are not unusual

A repeated letter means at least one character occurs two or more times in a word. In this Common subset, 779 of 2,365 five-letter words (32.9%) have a repeat. At seven letters, 1,832 of 2,680 (68.4%) do. The increase is understandable: longer words provide more positions in which a letter can recur. It does not mean those figures describe the probability of drawing a repeat in a seven-tile rack; letter-tile distributions and word formation rules are different.

Use this when solving a pattern rather than treating a duplicate as impossible. APPLE needs two Ps, LEVEL needs two Ls and two Es, and QUEUE repeats U and E. If your rack lacks a needed second copy, it cannot form that word without a blank. Try the Unscrambler with AELPP or LEEVL and check how the valid anagrams change when you remove a repeated letter.

Finding 2: two A/E/I/O/U vowels lead the five-letter subset

For this count, only A, E, I, O and U count as vowels; Y is deliberately treated separately. Of the 2,365 Common five-letter words, 1,336 have exactly two vowels (56.5%), 858 have one (36.3%), 161 have three (6.8%), five have no A/E/I/O/U vowels, and five have four. These are counts across a word list, not a recommended distribution for a guessing game's hidden answers.

If you have a five-letter clue with most letters still unknown, testing a balanced vowel–consonant structure is a reasonable way to explore the dictionary. But don't turn the percentage into a rigid rule. A correct answer may have one vowel, multiple occurrences of a vowel, or Y functioning as the vowel sound. CRANE and STARE illustrate two-vowel spelling patterns; QUEUE illustrates why repeated vowels can radically change the useful guesses.

Finding 3: beginning and ending letters behave differently

Within the Common five-letter subset, S starts 341 words (14.4%), while S ends 615 (26.0%). C starts 211 and B starts 186. For endings, E follows S with 319 words; Y is next with 266. This is partly influenced by forms such as plurals and conjugations in the list. A game with a restricted answer list may show a very different distribution.

These counts are more useful as a filtering tactic than as an answer prediction. If your crossword requires S as the first letter, browse five-letter words starting with S. If the final letter is certain, use the Crossword Solver with the fixed position. A fixed letter is evidence; a population-wide frequency is only a weak prior.

How to turn the findings into a practical search

  1. Choose the puzzle's actual constraint. A five-letter crossword with a fixed third letter is a different question from a rack of five movable tiles. Do not mix the two search methods.
  2. Begin with the smaller list. Common English is a more manageable first pass for ordinary puzzles. Broaden the dictionary only when familiar candidates fail.
  3. Account for repeats. Count each letter in your rack. A second E or T must actually be present; a question-mark wildcard represents only one missing tile.
  4. Use positional clues before frequency guesses. A confirmed beginning, ending or position narrows a list more reliably than assuming that S or two vowels must appear.
  5. Verify the target game's rules. The site is not the official Wordle answer list or a licensed tournament Scrabble dictionary. Its results are candidates, not rulings.

Reproducing the calculation

To check any number, read the public project's four Broad English length files, retain lowercase A–Z entries of the stated length, and intersect each set with the entries in data/common.txt. Count repeated-letter words with the rule new Set(word).size < word.length. For vowel totals, count characters in aeiou. For starts and ends, count the first and last letter separately. No external word-frequency corpus, paid dictionary or AI estimate is used in these figures.

We include the word-list source, analysis rules and snapshot date so readers can understand what the findings mean and what they do not mean. See which word list to choose for broader guidance, or how the word tools work for the underlying search behavior.

What this study cannot establish

It cannot tell you the next Wordle answer, the probability of winning a board game, how often letters occur in conversation, or whether any word is currently accepted by a particular official rule set. It also does not count answer frequency over time. If the project's lists change in a later release, these numbers should be recalculated rather than silently relabeled as newer.
