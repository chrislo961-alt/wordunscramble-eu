Using letter patterns in crosswords

A crossword solver is most useful after you have converted the clue into constraints. Answer length and crossing letters are hard facts; clue wording helps you rank the candidates that remain.

Start with exact answer length

Crossword grids already tell you how many letters the answer contains. That is the strongest first filter. If an answer has seven squares, do not search a general dictionary and scan mixed lengths; use a seven-character pattern immediately.

Add every confirmed crossing letter

Use a question mark for unknown squares and type known letters exactly where they appear. For example, ?r?a? represents a five-letter answer whose second letter is R and fourth letter is A. Each additional confirmed letter can reduce the candidate set dramatically.

Let clue grammar eliminate bad matches

If the clue is plural, a singular noun is unlikely unless wordplay changes the grammar. A clue written in the past tense often points to a past-tense answer. Comparative clues may suggest -er; superlatives may suggest -est. These are not absolute rules, but they are excellent ranking signals.

Watch abbreviations and proper names

Crosswords frequently use abbreviations, names and specialized vocabulary that a general English list may omit. If the pattern looks right but the solver finds nothing, do not force a near-match. Recheck the crossings and consider whether the clue signals an abbreviation, name, foreign word or theme entry.

Example: _ R _ A _

Suppose the clue implies a noun and crossing answers produce ?r?a?. A pattern search gives a finite candidate list. Instead of choosing the first familiar word, compare each result with the clue’s meaning and grammar. The pattern tells you what can fit; the clue tells you what should fit.

Use uncertain crossings cautiously

One wrong crossing letter can remove the correct answer completely. If a search returns nothing sensible, replace the least certain crossing with a question mark before broadening the dictionary. That is usually more productive than assuming the target word is obscure.
